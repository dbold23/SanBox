## Subject

**Sevengill pattern drift: a measurement your archive can make, and the analysis back to you**

## Body (288 words)

Dear [NAME],

I am Daniel Sambold at CSU Monterey Bay. I would like to ask for access to one defined slice of the
Sevengill Shark ID Project archive, and to say up front what you get back.

Nobody has measured how fast a sevengill's freckle pattern drifts; there is no published study. Yours is
the strongest catalogue for answering it: La Jolla since 2010, a decade of human-confirmed matches on
the freckle pattern itself. (Two others could be asked, in South Africa and New Zealand; neither covers
this population.) The answer is useful to you first: it tells you how far back a match can still be
trusted.

The ask: the confirmed-resight subset. Every individual with two or more confirmed encounters, and for
each encounter the image, the individual ID, the date, the left/right viewpoint, and any size estimate —
plus, if Sharkbook records it, whether each match was found by eye or off an algorithm suggestion, which
we need to keep the result honest.
Contributor copyright unchanged, held under an agreement, no redistribution, and no image published
without the photographer's permission.

What you get back: a recapture-gap curve — match strength against elapsed years — with the size and
gallery-size confounds controlled; a per-individual report ranking your weakest multi-year links,
directly actionable in your matching queue; the export-to-analysis tooling so you can re-run it each
season without us; and co-authorship on anything published, on your terms.

Why we are worth the trouble: we ran this protocol first on a 9,861-individual PIT-tagged wild-fish
catalogue — a completed three-round pre-registered campaign that found our own leading hypothesis wrong.
It is not published yet; the full internal report is yours on request. The measurement plan is attached,
pre-registered before seeing your data.

Glad to talk any time.

Daniel Sambold
CSU Monterey Bay — `dsambold@csumb.edu`

## Notes for the sender (not part of the email)

* **Do not ask for "the archive."** The ask is deliberately a named subset with a named purpose. A
  citizen-science non-profit whose value is contributor trust cannot say yes to an open-ended request,
  and should not be asked to.
* **Do not quote "~76 individuals"** back at them. That figure is advocacy/press-grade `[SEARCH]` and
  they hold the real number.
* **The offer must survive a null result.** If the drift measurement comes back RED (see
  `DRIFT_PROTOCOL.md` §5.3), they still get the per-individual report and the tooling. Say so if asked;
  do not imply the deliverable is contingent on a favourable answer.
* **Nothing of ours is published.** The Melops campaign is a completed, pre-registered, internal
  three-round campaign with a written report; it has not been submitted anywhere. The letter says so.
  Do not let a later draft slide back into "published" — offering the report on request is the honest
  version and it is also the more useful offer.
* **"Strongest", not "only".** Their catalogue is the best-positioned archive for this question, not the
  only one that could answer it — `DRIFT_PROTOCOL.md` §1 names the fallbacks (Sharkbook's South African
  sevengill data; Otago's Stewart Island BUV catalogue) and the attached protocol is in their hands. A
  letter that claims uniqueness while its own attachment lists alternatives reads as either careless or
  manipulative, and is the sort of thing a partner remembers.
* If they cannot share images at all, the fallback ask is **derived data only**: per-encounter
  individual ID, date, viewpoint, size estimate, and the incumbent's own match-score history. That still
  supports a reduced version of §5 (similarity decay from their scores) and it costs them almost
  nothing — keep it in the back pocket rather than in the first email.
